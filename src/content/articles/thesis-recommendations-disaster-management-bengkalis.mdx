## Reading the evidence correctly

The discussion chapter spends as much effort on how to read the numbers as on the numbers. A few of its points matter for anyone planning around them.

**ENSO acts through local drought.** Adding SPEI-3 to the count model cuts the ONI effect from 1.95 to 1.29, and each unit increase in SPEI-3 lowers expected hotspots by about 65% (IRR 0.35). The thesis calls this attenuation, not a formal mediation analysis, and uses "mediated" as shorthand. In practice, a local drought indicator carries more of the actionable information than ENSO itself, while ENSO remains valuable as a long-lead precursor of drought. The total ENSO effect held up under year-clustered errors and a year-level wild bootstrap, but its direct effect after drought was not significant under year-level inference.

**The fire season is bimodal.** February–April contributes more hotspots than June–September (the abstract gives February–March 43.4% and June–September 38.4%). The conventional dry-season framing, June to September, misses the early-year peak that follows the northeast monsoon and can be severe in El Niño years such as 2015 and 2019.

**Landscape decides where climate-driven risk is realised.** The significant heterogeneity of the ENSO response, the concentration of hotspots in Rupat and Bandar Laksamana, the over-representation of moderately deep peat and the canal result all point the same way. The thesis also uses Mandau as a lesson in why formal testing matters: with little peat (5,655 ha), a high oil palm fraction and only 22 hotspots, its weak ONI correlation reflects low power, and a model that never saw Mandau predicted it well (AUC 0.89).

**Machine learning adds less than it appears to.** Under spatial validation, climate carries the model (pooled AUC falls from 0.795 to 0.703 when climate is removed; a static-only model reaches 0.624). Under temporal validation, the largest gains come from static sub-district features and fire persistence, which act largely as sub-district identifiers. On held-out predictions climate variables carried 59.1% of the mean absolute SHAP value, against 21.8% for anthropogenic, 9.7% for biophysical-static and 9.3% for seasonal features. The thesis stresses that SHAP is a second view of the same model, not an independent test.

## Five recommendations

1. **Adopt a two-window preparedness calendar** (February–April and July–August) for Bengkalis, instead of a single June–September dry season.
2. **Use ONI forecasts to escalate preparedness** in fire months when El Niño is expected, and monitor SPEI-3 (or a groundwater-level proxy) as the near-term trigger.
3. **Prioritise canal blocking, rewetting and patrols** in the sub-districts with the highest hotspot burden (Rupat, Bandar Laksamana, Bengkalis, Pinggir) and on non-forest peat within a few hundred metres of canals.
4. **Integrate daily FIRMS monitoring with the DBSCAN-based filter** to reduce false alarms from industrial sources around Duri.
5. **Communicate model skill honestly.** Risk maps and alert levels should state their uncertainty and their performance relative to a seasonal baseline.

The fifth is the one that is easiest to skip and probably the most important. The skill test in [the early-warning post](/blog/early-warning-skill-bengkalis) is the reason: a warning that looks sophisticated but does no better than the calendar should be labelled as such.

### How this fits the legal framework

The thesis grounds the table in Indonesia's disaster law. Under Law No. 24 of 2007, disaster management is organised into pre-disaster (prevention, mitigation, preparedness and early warning), emergency-response and post-disaster phases. Three emergency statuses exist: alert (*siaga darurat*), emergency response (*tanggap darurat*) and transition to recovery, each declared for a fixed period by the governor at provincial level and the regent at regency level, on the recommendation of the disaster agency. Riau Provincial Regulation No. 1 of 2019 gives the BPBD the duty to install and test early warning systems and to prepare the determination of alert status.

Provincial alert periods in Riau typically open in February and close at the end of the second dry season, which already matches the bimodal regime. That is why the seasonal calendar is the right reference for the skill test, and why recommendation 1 is more a formalisation of practice than a departure from it.

## What the thesis does not claim

The limitations section lists eleven. Four bear directly on how far the recommendations can be pushed:

- **A short record.** Ten years contain only two to three ENSO warm episodes (2015, 2019 with a positive IOD, 2023), so confidence intervals for ENSO effects remain optimistic.
- **Hotspots are a proxy.** They measure detected active fire, not burned area, and filtering affects inter-annual retention (28–71%). The validation against burned area is partial and affected by cloud cover.
- **Static covariates.** Land-use variables are constant per sub-district, partly computed over the whole period, and highly collinear.
- **Climate data not bias-corrected.** CHIRPS and ERA5-Land were not validated against station data because no representative station exists (the nearest BMKG station identified was Karimun, about 120 km away).

The analysis also speaks mainly to the hazard component of risk: where and when fire is likely. It says little about exposure of people and assets or about local response capacity, and an early-warning system is more than the forecast that the skill scores assess.

## What comes next

The thesis lists five directions for future research:

1. Extend the record backwards (for example 2001–2024 with MODIS) to include more ENSO events such as 2006 and 2009.
2. Replace static covariates with time-varying land-use and groundwater-level data, such as peat water-table monitoring networks.
3. Complete burned-area validation with cloud-robust approaches (for example Sentinel-1 SAR) and calibrate dATBI thresholds for tropical peat.
4. Develop probabilistic sub-seasonal forecasts that link ENSO forecasts to SPEI predictions, evaluated on out-of-time years (2025 onward).
5. Apply geographically weighted regression or hierarchical Bayesian models to quantify spatially varying ENSO effects with appropriate uncertainty.
