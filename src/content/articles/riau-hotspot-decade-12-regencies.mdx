Riau holds most of Sumatra's peat and most of its smoke. This post summarises a regional report that compares monthly hotspots, rainfall, land surface temperature and the Oceanic Niño Index across all twelve regencies and cities from 2015 to 2024. The full interactive version, with a map, per-regency charts and the correlation statistics, is on the companion site.

<RiauYears client:visible />

2015 alone had 9,388 hotspots, 30% of the decade. 2019 followed with 7,935. After 2019 the annual count fell back to between roughly 700 and 1,800, with 2022 the lowest at 688.

### Why those two years

2015 was exceptional well beyond Riau. Fire carbon emissions across maritime Southeast Asia were the largest since 1997,[^huijnen16] and the season was the most severe in the NASA satellite record, with fire on degraded peat remaining nonlinearly sensitive to dry conditions.[^field16] 2019 coincided with an extreme positive Indian Ocean Dipole that reduced rainfall over Indonesia and favoured peat fires in southern Sumatra,[^iskandar22] a reminder that ENSO is not the only ocean signal behind a bad year. A modelling study of Riau over 2015–2021 found widespread, prolonged extreme fire hazard only in 2015 and 2019, and fewer extreme events after peat rewetting began;[^taufik23] that fits the post-2019 drop but is modelled hazard, not observed fire, so it does not establish the cause.

Fire in this part of Sumatra is also not purely a drought story. In June 2013, in a year wetter than average after a two-month dry spell, fires in Central Sumatra burned 163,336 ha, 84% of it peat.[^gaveau14] A hotspot record read only through the climate indices would underweight episodes like that. For scale, a machine-learning study of Sumatran peat vulnerability using 2012–2019 hotspot data ranked Riau as the most fire-prone province.[^prasetyo22]

## Where

<ConfidenceTable client:visible />

The default view is the report's ≥30% threshold, which in MODIS terms keeps the nominal (30–79%) and high (80–100%) confidence classes and drops the low class.[^modisguide] The confidence value is a heuristic combining several sub-confidences, not a probability of fire.[^giglio16] Switch the view to see how the picture changes if you keep only higher-confidence detections. Five regencies hold 73% of the ≥30% detections. Bengkalis is second, which is why the next posts zoom into it.

Counts by regency say where detections fall, not why. Three published results help frame the question. Across Sumatra and Kalimantan, peatlands cover about 13% of the land, yet their annual burned share in 2001–2018 was roughly five times that of non-peat areas.[^vetrita19] Concessions accounted for about 41% of Sumatra's fire emissions in the 2006 burning season, 62% of that from peat.[^marlier15] And the land itself has changed: across the peatlands of Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra and Borneo combined, peat swamp forest fell from 76% of the area in 1990 to 29% in 2015 while managed land rose from 11% to 50%.[^miettinen16] A regency-level table cannot separate these effects, which is one reason the Bengkalis posts move to finer units.

Local rainfall is the strongest single correlate, and the ocean index the weakest. The Bengkalis study finds the same thing at finer scale: local drought tracks fire more closely than the ocean index does.

### Reading the correlations with care

**The p-values overstate the evidence.** The 1,320 regency-months are not 1,320 independent observations. Neighbouring regencies share weather, and ONI is a single series copied into every regency, so for ONI the effective sample is closer to 120 months than to 1,320. Treating dependent observations as replicates is the pseudoreplication problem,[^hurlbert84] and with n this large almost any correlation reaches p below 0.001. The coefficients are still informative as effect sizes; the significance statements are not. Pooling can also hide opposite behaviour inside units, the pattern known as Simpson's paradox,[^simpson51] and no within-regency check is reported for these twelve regencies.

**The "SPI proxy" is a proxy for good reason.** The WMO user guide for the Standardized Precipitation Index recommends a record of at least thirty years, and the programme's summary says as little as twenty can be used.[^svoboda12] A ten-year record standardised against itself is dominated by 2015 and 2019, so the proxy measures anomalies relative to a decade that includes two extreme seasons.

**Window length matters, and so does region.** Fanin and van der Werf found that the rainfall window that best tracked annual fire was 120 days in southern Sumatra and Kalimantan but 30 days in northern Sumatra.[^fanin17] The monthly rainfall used here is the shortest option, so a longer accumulation could correlate more tightly in some regencies. Likewise, Aldrian and Susanto found that Indonesian rainfall regions differ in how strongly ENSO shapes them, with a weak signal in the north-western region and strong effects on June–September rainfall in the southern and eastern regions.[^aldrian03] A single pooled ONI coefficient of +0.23 averages over that heterogeneity.

The Bengkalis posts that follow use CHIRPS rainfall[^funk15] and ERA5-Land temperature.[^munoz21] The regional report does not name its sources, which is why the provenance note below matters.
