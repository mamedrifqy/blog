Every result in this study rests on one assumption: that a satellite *hotspot* is a fair stand-in for a peat fire. A hotspot is a heat anomaly in a pixel roughly a kilometre across, seen at one moment.[^giglio16] It is not a burn scar. Reviewers of the thesis asked, reasonably, what evidence there is that the two line up. This post is the answer the revised analysis gives, and it is a mixed one.

## The short version

- **Where and when: hotspots hold up.** Month by month and kecamatan by kecamatan they agree with the MODIS burned-area product clearly better than chance, and year by year the two track each other (Spearman ρ = 0.73 across 110 kecamatan-years).
- **How much: they do not.** Burned hectares per hotspot vary from about 5 to 28 depending on the year and the product. Hotspot counts are a signal of fire activity, not an area estimate. A study at a degraded peat swamp forest in Central Kalimantan reached the same conclusion.[^tansey08]
- **Small fire years are the weak spot.** In 2023, more than half of the burned area detected by Landsat had no hotspot within 2 km.
- **The climate signal is weaker in burned area than in hotspots.** The correlation of monthly burned area with ONI is 0.09, against 0.24 for hotspots. The direction agrees; the strength does not.

## Check 1: do hotspot months match burned months?

The first test needs no new satellite work. MODIS publishes a monthly burned-area product, MCD64A1, at 500 m.[^giglio18] For each of the 1,320 kecamatan-months (11 kecamatan × 120 months) the question is simple: did the study's filtered hotspots flag a fire, and did MCD64A1 see at least one burned pixel?

Across the regency, 327 months carry a hotspot and 155 carry burned pixels. Precision is 0.32, recall is 0.68 and Cohen's κ is 0.33, a fair but unspectacular agreement. Allowing the burn to be dated one month either side raises precision to 0.54 and recall to 0.83, which says a good part of the disagreement is timing: a hotspot is a snapshot, a burn scar appears when the algorithm sees the surface change.

<Tier1Map client:visible />

The map shows how uneven that is. Bantan and Pinggir recover more than 90% of their burned months with a hotspot. Bukit Batu is the outlier: it has 22 burned months and 20 hotspot months, but they rarely coincide (recall 0.18, ρ = 0.05). Mandau and Bathin Solapan have too few burned months (2 and 4) for their ratios to mean much.

Over a full year the picture tightens. Annual hotspot counts and burned hectares correlate at ρ = 0.73 across the 110 kecamatan-years, and at 0.65 on average *within* a kecamatan. There is one more warning in the diagnostics: in 23 of the 83 kecamatan-months with at least 10 hotspots, MCD64A1 shows no burned pixel at all, 10 of them in 2015.

The dependence is documented, not just suspected. The MCD64 user guide states that the product is generated from MODIS surface reflectance *and* active-fire inputs,[^mcd64guide] and the algorithm paper reports that 44% of burned cells coincide with an active fire on the same day, and 68% within two days.[^giglio18] Agreement between the two products here therefore shows that they are consistent with each other; it does not show that either is right.

## Check 2: a burn index from Landsat, with no fire detections in it

For independent evidence the notebook follows Bilal (2025), who built an index for burned-area mapping that uses reflectance alone:[^bilal25] **ATBI = NBR × (SWIR / NIR)**, where NBR = (NIR − SWIR) / (NIR + SWIR). Burn is mapped from the *drop* in ATBI between a pre-fire image and post-fire images (dATBI ≥ 0.20 marks a burned pixel), at Landsat's 30 m resolution. The standard dNBR is run alongside it for comparison.

Applying a method from a paper to a tropical peat swamp means deviating from it, and the notebook lists the deviations plainly:

- **Atmospheric correction.** The paper uses SREM; no code was released, so Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 (LaSRC) is used for both indices. Results may differ slightly from the paper's version, which itself reports cleaner output from SREM-corrected reflectance than from the standard LaSRC product.[^bilal25]
- **Pre-fire image.** A single clear scene is almost never available in this climate, so the pre-fire reference is a median composite. Two windows are tested: the wet season of the same year, or the dry season of the year before. The first risks a wet-to-dry seasonal bias; the second can count last year's scars as "already burned".
- **Thresholds.** 0.20 comes from the paper and was calibrated on non-peat ecosystems. Higher values (0.27, 0.44, 0.66) are tested as a sensitivity grid.
- **A matched control.** dATBI and dNBR use the same thresholds, so a higher confirmation rate could just mean more false alarms. Each hotspot is therefore compared with control points on peat in the same kecamatan and month, and what is reported is the *difference* in hit-rate, not the raw hit-rate.

### Does the index tell burned ground from unburned ground?

Pick a year, a buffer and a pre-fire window below. A good index should hit near hotspots much more often than at control points. It should also report a plausible burned area; the peat in Bengkalis covers about 511,000 ha.

<LiftExplorer client:visible />

Two things are worth taking from this explorer.

**dATBI separates hotspots from controls in every combination.** At the paper's threshold of 0.20 and a 500 m buffer, the hit-rate at hotspots exceeds that at control points by 0.56 to 0.63. With a 1 km buffer and the dry-season pre-fire window, hotspots hit 0.84, 0.82 and 0.81 of the time in 2015, 2019 and 2023, against 0.27, 0.18 and 0.09 for controls. The result barely moves between the two pre-fire windows.

**Standard dNBR does not, at the usual threshold.** At T = 0.20 its hit-rate at control points is already 94–99%, so there is almost nothing left to separate: the difference is at most 0.13 (500 m) and usually near zero. It also maps 88,000 to 189,000 ha as burned, which is not plausible for a year like 2023 that had 83 hotspots. At T = 0.66 dNBR does separate hotspots from controls, but with the dry-season window it then reports 19,014 ha burned in 2023, eight times the MODIS figure for that year. That is difficult to reconcile with a quiet fire year, so dNBR serves here only as a comparison.

## Check 3: how much of the burned area do hotspots account for?

The last question flips the first. Instead of asking whether hotspots land on burn scars, ask how much of the Landsat burned area has a hotspot nearby. A high share could be a fluke if hotspots happened to cover a large part of the regency, so the notebook always compares it to a baseline: the fraction of the regency that lies within the same distance of any hotspot.

<ExplainedShare client:visible />

At 1 km the study's filtered hotspots account for 82% of 2015's burned area, 73% of 2019's and 46% of 2023's, which is 8, 11 and 38 times what chance would give. Two points stand out.

**The DBSCAN filter costs little where fires were large.** Using all raw detections raises the share at 1 km by 4 points in 2015, 9 in 2019 and 3 in 2023. The filter does discard some points that sat next to real burn scars, but it also removes false alarms, and the trade-off is modest.

**Small years are poorly covered.** In 2023 the share stays at about 47–52% even at a 2 km radius, regardless of which hotspot set is used. That is the Landsat burn that MODIS hotspots do not see at all: roughly 420 ha of the 774 ha detected that year, with no filtered hotspot within a kilometre. Small, short-lived peat fires are exactly what a coarse thermal sensor misses: the MODIS fire-detection validation reports high omission for small fires,[^giglio16] and small fires are known to add materially to burned area in this part of the world.[^randerson12]

### Is it just the land cover?

Hotspots do not fall on a random sample of peat. Under hotspots, ESA WorldCover shows grass in 25% (2015) and 36% (2019) of cases, against 10% and 15% under control points, and tree cover is correspondingly lower. If burn detection were easier on grassland, that could flatter the result. Re-running the comparison with controls matched to the hotspots' own land-cover class leaves the difference almost unchanged: 0.57 against 0.58 in 2015, 0.52 against 0.56 in 2019 and 0.54 against 0.58 in 2023, all at 500 m.

## What the burned-area signal says about ENSO and drought

The headline finding of the thesis is that El Niño and drought raise fire activity. The same test run on burned area gives the same signs but weaker numbers.

Drought is the stronger driver in both records: at the annual scale, July–October SPI-3 tracks the hotspot total at ρ = −0.82 and the MODIS burned area at −0.55 (ten years, so treat both with care). The gap is consistent with burned area being a noisier, coarser measure, and also with hotspots responding to dry conditions more readily than burn scars do.

## Reading these numbers against the published validation work

Most checks above are agreement tests between two products. The literature lets us ask a harder question: is this level of agreement what we should expect, given what is known about each product's errors?

### MODIS burned area is an imperfect reference, especially here

The global validation of MCD64A1 summarised in its user guide gives omission of 72.6%, commission of 40.2% and a burned area biased low by 54.1%, mostly because small burns are missed.[^mcd64guide] A dedicated validation against 558 Landsat 8 image pairs found tropical forest to be one of three biomes with omission above 90% and commission above 50%.[^boschetti19] Work in Indonesia is a little kinder. Against SPOT 5 reference data, MCD64A1 Collection 6 was the best of the four MODIS products tested; the products other than MCD45A1 detected 38–48% of burned area, and agreement was better on peat (R² 0.6–0.8) than off peat (0.2–0.5), with cloud and smoke reducing accuracy.[^vetrita21]

Two consequences follow for Check 1. First, a burned month that MCD64A1 missed never enters the test, and a burned month it invented counts against the hotspots, so recall of 0.68 and precision of 0.32 measure agreement with an imperfect map, not accuracy. Second, scale matters. The Landsat validation found that fine-scale errors largely offset at 3–6 km resolution,[^boschetti19] and a kecamatan-month is a much coarser unit than a 500 m pixel. Presence or absence of any burned pixel in a kecamatan-month forgives a lot of pixel-level error, so the figures here should not be compared with pixel-level accuracy numbers.

### What κ = 0.33 hides

Pontius and Millones argue that kappa should be replaced by two quantities: disagreement in *how much* is mapped, and disagreement in *where*.[^pontius11] The post's counts allow a rough version. From 327 hotspot months at precision 0.32 and 155 burned months at recall 0.68, about 105 months agree, which leaves roughly 222 hotspot-only months and 50 burned-only months. Over 1,320 kecamatan-months that is a quantity disagreement of about 13% and an allocation disagreement of about 8% (my arithmetic from the reported precision and recall, not an output of the notebook). Most of the disagreement is therefore that hotspots flag more months than MCD64A1 maps, not that the two flag different months. That fits the omission rates above and the improvement from a one-month slack, and it is a more useful description than a single κ.

### Hectares per hotspot

Dividing the table's burned areas by the filtered hotspot counts gives MCD64A1 values of about 7 ha (2015), 15 ha (2019) and 28 ha (2023) per hotspot, and dATBI values of about 5, 5 and 9 ha. In Central Kalimantan, Tansey and colleagues found about 15–16 ha per hotspot, with omission near 60% and commission near 8% at that site, and concluded that hotspot count is a weak, vegetation-dependent proxy for burned area.[^tansey08] The Bengkalis range brackets their figure, which is reassuring about order of magnitude and not at all about stability: the MCD64A1 ratio moves roughly fourfold between years. The year with the fewest hotspots (2023) has the most hectares per hotspot in both products. One reading is that when fires are small and scattered the thermal sensor catches only some of them, which inflates the ratio.[^giglio16] The notebook does not test this, so it stays a hypothesis.

### The small-fire gap

Randerson and colleagues estimate that small fires add about 35% to global burned area, with the largest relative increases in Equatorial Asia.[^randerson12] That is consistent with the roughly 420 ha of Landsat burn in 2023 that had no filtered hotspot within a kilometre. A finer active-fire product, VIIRS at 375 m, exists,[^schroeder14] and repeating Check 3 with it is the obvious test of whether the gap is a resolution problem. It also matters beyond the study window: the 2013 Central Sumatra fires burned 163,336 ha, 84% of it peat, in a year wetter than average after a two-month dry spell.[^gaveau14] A hotspot record read only through ENSO would underweight such episodes.

### Landsat check: what the index paper does and does not support

Bilal reports, over 50 wildfire events worldwide, precision of about 0.92 for dATBI against 0.73 for dNBR and F1 of about 0.94 against 0.84, scored against random-forest reference classifications.[^bilal25] The explorer's result that dNBR cannot separate hotspots from controls at the usual threshold, while dATBI can, points the same way. But the evidence is of a different kind. The paper scores a classification against a reference map; this study asks whether hotspots sit on burned ground more often than matched controls do. Neither is a validation on tropical peat. A design-based accuracy assessment in the sense of Olofsson and colleagues, with a probability sample of reference pixels interpreted independently, would be needed for that.[^olofsson14]

For 2019, the dATBI-to-MODIS ratio tracked the number of usable post-fire scenes. Roy and colleagues describe a combined Landsat-8 and Sentinel-2 change-detection approach to burned-area mapping,[^roy19] and adding a second sensor's scenes is the natural way to test whether coverage explains the weak 2019 agreement.

### Why burned area may show a weaker climate signal

Burned area correlates with ONI at 0.09, against 0.24 for hotspots. One candidate explanation comes from the Indonesian validation above: cloud and smoke reduced the accuracy of MODIS burned-area products.[^vetrita21] Smoke is heaviest in dry El Niño seasons, so detection error could be correlated with the very signal being measured and would weaken the apparent relationship. This is a hypothesis, not a result. The peat-only control design also matters here, since in Sumatra and Kalimantan the annual burned share per unit area on peat has been about five times that of non-peat land.[^vetrita19] Finally, hectares are not emissions: carbon loss per hectare in drained peatland fell from 114 to 64, 38 and 13 t C/ha over the first to fourth fire,[^konecny16] so an unstable hectares-per-hotspot ratio and a recurrence-dependent loss per hectare compound each other.

## What this means for the thesis

<div class="pp-callout"><div>
<p><strong>Safe to say:</strong> in Bengkalis, 2015–2024, hotspots are a defensible indicator of <em>where and when peat fires were active</em>. Landsat, which shares no fire-detection inputs with them, confirms that hotspots sit on burned ground far more often than matched peat controls do.</p>
<p><strong>Not safe to say:</strong> that hotspot counts measure <em>how much</em> burned. Hectares per hotspot range from about 5 to 28 across years and products, and small fire years are largely invisible to the thermal record.</p>
<p><strong>Open:</strong> why dATBI and MODIS disagree so much in 2019, how the dATBI threshold behaves on peat (it was calibrated elsewhere), and whether the weaker climate signal in burned area reflects the data or the fires.</p>
</div></div>
