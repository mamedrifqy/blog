Earlier posts here mostly ask how each kecamatan's fire record correlates with ENSO. A thesis on *spatiotemporal* fire has to say more: is fire getting better or worse, does it cluster in space, does it burst in identifiable events, and how far ahead can the climate tell you? The revised notebook adds a battery of tests for exactly these questions. This post goes through them, including the ones that returned nothing.

## The short version

- **Fire declined over the decade, but the decade starts with its worst year.** 32% of all filtered hotspots fall in 2015 alone.
- **There is no hotspot cluster to find.** Global <Term id="morans-i">Moran's I</Term> is negative (neighbours differ), no kecamatan has a significant local cluster label over the whole decade, and no kecamatan is a persistent, intensifying or diminishing hotspot.
- **Fire arrives in bursts.** Large events last from days to weeks, and most of the biggest begin in the Feb–Mar dry spell, not at the main June–September peak.
- **Lead times differ by place.** Rupat and Rupat Utara respond to <Term id="oni-phases">ONI</Term> immediately; Talang Muandau and Mandau at the longest lag tested. Local drought (<Term id="spei-3">SPEI-3</Term>) is often the tighter and faster signal.

## Is fire fading?

Ten years of annual totals tell the first part of the story.

<AnnualBars client:visible />

2015 holds 32% of the decade's hotspots, 2019 another 25%, and no year after 2020 exceeds 150 (2021: 148; 2022: 23; 2023: 83; 2024: 88). For scale, regional modelling put fire carbon emissions in maritime Southeast Asia in 2015 at the highest level since 1997.[^huijnen16] The formal test is a **seasonal <Term id="mann-kendall">Mann-Kendall</Term>** trend test,[^hirsch84] an extension of Mann's rank-based trend test,[^mann45] on each kecamatan's 120 monthly counts, which compares each month with the same calendar month in other years, so the dry-season cycle does not masquerade as a trend. Switch the map below to the trend view.

<TrendMap client:visible />

Ten kecamatan decline significantly, with Kendall's τ between −0.11 (Rupat Utara) and −0.27 (Siak Kecil). Mandau is the exception, with τ = −0.02 (p = 0.59), but it has the fewest hotspots of all (22), so it has the least to decline from.

<div class="pp-callout note"><div>
<p><strong>Read "fading" with care.</strong> A downward trend that begins at the largest event in the record is partly a statement about 2015. Mann-Kendall also reports a slope per month, which comes out as 0 in every kecamatan here (most months have no hotspots at all), so the test says *that* counts are falling but not by how much. The notebook does not separate the effect of the 2015 outlier from the later years. And 2023–24 was an El Niño period too; fire stayed low. The study does not test why.</p>
</div></div>

That last point matters for early warning. If ENSO phase alone set the level of fire, 2023–24 should have looked more like 2015. It did not. Whatever changed between those years (land management, rewetting, the weather within the phase) is not in the data this study uses. Published work gives several candidate explanations; they are set out in the section on the wider literature below.

## Does fire cluster in space?

The standard tool is Moran's I,[^moran50] which asks whether neighbouring kecamatan have similar fire levels. The answer, for mean monthly hotspots, is **I = −0.45 (p = 0.037)**: a significant tendency for neighbours to *differ*, not to resemble each other. With eleven units, two of them an island pair, that is a statement about contrast between kecamatan, not about clusters. The same test on the strength of each kecamatan's ONI correlation gives I = +0.17 (p = 0.20).

**<Term id="lisa">Local Moran</Term>'s I (LISA)**[^anselin95] classifies each kecamatan as part of a high-high cluster, a low-low cluster, or a spatial outlier. For the whole decade none is significant (all p ≥ 0.36). The earlier run had flagged Rupat Utara as low-high; with the final hotspot list it does not survive. In the neighbour structure used here, Rupat and Rupat Utara are each other's *only* neighbours, so the pair can only ever say "these two island kecamatan differ a lot", and there is no cluster of hot places around them.

Splitting the months by ENSO phase does not reveal one either. Moran's I is −0.25 in El Niño (44 months, p = 0.26), +0.09 in La Niña (30 months, p = 0.25) and −0.14 in neutral months (46, p = 0.49). The spatial structure of fire does not measurably change with the phase.

### Hotspot or not, period by period

The **emerging hotspot** classification repeats the LISA test in five two-year blocks and looks for a consistent pattern: persistent, intensifying, diminishing, sporadic.

<EmergingGrid client:visible />

Six of 55 kecamatan-periods carry a significant label, and they do not repeat. Ten kecamatan end up as "no pattern"; Bathin Solapan, the only high-high cell (2017–18), is "sporadic". The honest reading is that, with eleven large polygons, the test has little room to detect structure. Fire in Bengkalis is concentrated in a few kecamatan (Rupat alone has 976 of the 3,402 hotspots), but that is a difference in *level*, not a spatial cluster of the kind these statistics find.

## Fire arrives in events

Monthly counts blur what happens on the ground. **<Term id="st-dbscan">ST-DBSCAN</Term>**[^birant07] clusters hotspots that are close in both space and time, extending the density-based <Term id="dbscan">DBSCAN</Term> algorithm:[^ester96] here within 1.5 km and 30 days of each other, in groups of at least 3. It was run on the 2,292 high-confidence hotspots (confidence ≥ 70%), a cut that sits inside the product's "nominal" class (30–79%), whose class boundaries the user guide defines at 30 and 80.[^modisguide]

It finds **136 events** and puts 90.3% of the hotspots into one. The remaining 222 are singletons. 30 events have 20 or more hotspots. The ten largest are shown here against the ENSO phase of each month.

<EventTimeline client:visible />

Three details stand out:

- **Size and duration are not the same.** Event 4 (67 hotspots) lasted five days in February 2015; event 9, started two weeks later, ran for 55 days. A fire front that moves quickly looks very different on a map from a smouldering peat fire that reignites.
- **2015 and 2019 dominate**, both in El Niño-phase months (2019 was also a year of an extreme positive Indian Ocean Dipole, which ONI does not capture[^iskandar22]), but four of the ten largest come from other years: July–August 2018 (neutral), late March to early May 2016 (the tail of the 2015–16 El Niño), February–March 2020 (a weak El Niño, ONI around 0.6) and March 2021 (La Niña).
- **The season is early.** Eight of the ten began between 31 January and 29 March, in the first dry spell of the year. Only two began in June–July. The MODIS burned-area record points the same way: in 2015 and 2019 it peaks in February–March, and in 2015 again in July. A warning system built only around the June–September dry season would miss the start of eight of the ten largest events.

## How early can the climate warn?

Each kecamatan was correlated with ONI and SPEI-3 at lags of 0 to 6 months. The best lag for each is on the map (switch the view above).

- **ONI** correlations run from ρ = +0.18 (Rupat Utara) to +0.43 (Siak Kecil). Optimal lead times range from 0 months (Rupat, Bukit Batu) through 3–4 (Siak Kecil, Bengkalis, Pinggir) to 5–6 (Bantan, Bathin Solapan, Talang Muandau, Mandau).
- **SPEI-3** correlations run from −0.17 (Pinggir) to −0.43 (Siak Kecil). They are stronger than ONI's in 7 of 11 kecamatan, and in 9 of 11 the best lag is zero. Local drought tells you what is happening now; ONI tells you what may be coming. SPEI is a drought index that adds temperature-driven evaporative demand to a rainfall-based one,[^vicente10] which makes it a more proximate measure of the surface water balance than an ocean index.

Two cautions. The search stopped at 6 months, and the two longest optima (Mandau and Talang Muandau, both on ONI) sit on that boundary, so the true best lag could be longer. And the correlation at neighbouring lags is usually close to the maximum: a kecamatan with "best lag 5" is better read as "ONI informs fire anywhere from a few months to half a year ahead". The notebook turns the ONI lags into per-kecamatan watch windows ("alert N months before the dry-season peak"), which is a reasonable operational summary but not a precise forecast.

The chain from ocean to fire also shows in the phase statistics. Grouping months by ENSO phase explains 27% of the variance in SPEI-3 (a large effect), 7% in rainfall, 7% in air temperature and 6% in hotspot counts. ENSO shapes the *drought*; the drought, with all the local factors, shapes the fire.

## One more check: is the regency-wide relationship an artefact?

Pooling eleven kecamatan can reverse a relationship that holds in every kecamatan, or create one that holds in none: Simpson's paradox.[^simpson51] The check is to compute the same correlation inside each unit and compare signs.

<SimpsonDots client:visible />

All eleven kecamatan have the same sign as the pooled value, and the relationship is strongest in Rupat (−0.46) and Siak Kecil (−0.45). Mandau is weakest at −0.15. The pooled rainfall-fire relationship is not a mixing artefact.

## Reading the space-time results against the literature

### A short record, and what a trend test can claim

The seasonal Mann-Kendall test compares each month only with the same calendar month in other years. Hirsch and Slack found that its Type I error stays reliable under serial correlation, except with strong long-term persistence or very short records of about five years.[^hirsch84] Ten years is above that line but not far above it, so the p-values are best read as indicative. A second issue is independence. Ten of eleven kecamatan declining is not eleven independent confirmations: they share the same weather and the same enormous 2015, so the result is closer to one fact seen eleven times, the usual problem of treating non-independent observations as replicates.[^hurlbert84] Two follow-up runs would be informative: the same test with 2015 excluded, and on 2016 onward. Both are limited by the short record, but a decline that survives them is a decline that is not only about the first year.

### Why a decade can "fade" without anything changing

Field and colleagues found that fire on degraded peat remains nonlinearly sensitive to dry conditions, with 2015 the most severe season in the NASA satellite record.[^field16] If the response is nonlinear, a decade that opens with an extreme drought and is followed by milder ones will show a steep decline in fire even if the underlying sensitivity never changed. That is the null hypothesis against which any management story needs to be set.

There is also a management hypothesis. Taufik and colleagues coupled a weather model with a drought-fire model for Riau over 2015–2021 and found that extreme fire-hazard events fell from seven before peat rewetting to four after, with the affected area down by about 5% and event duration up; widespread, prolonged extremes occurred only in 2015 and 2019.[^taufik23] That is modelled hazard, not observed fire, and it covers Riau rather than Bengkalis, so it is a lead and not an explanation. It does show that the data used here (hotspot counts, climate indices) cannot separate "less fire because of less drought" from "less fire because of rewetting", a gap the post's caveat already points to.

### Eleven polygons and the scale of clustering

Moran's I of −0.45 (p = 0.037) comes from only eleven units, two of which are each other's only neighbours. With so few values the p-value is fragile, and a negative I here mostly reflects Rupat's high counts beside its quiet island neighbour; it is not evidence of any cluster. The LISA results carry a multiple-testing problem on top. At a 5% level, 55 kecamatan-periods would throw up about three significant labels by chance alone; six were found, and they did not repeat. Local statistics at neighbouring locations also share observations, so the tests are not independent.[^anselin95]

The more useful observation is that the notebook contradicts itself, productively, across scales. At the kecamatan level there is no spatial structure; at 1.5 km and 30 days, ST-DBSCAN puts 90.3% of hotspots into events. Fire in Bengkalis is clustered at the scale of a fire and not at the scale of an administrative unit. The Simpson check shares this limit. A within-kecamatan correlation is still an aggregate: Robinson showed long ago that correlations computed on areal units need not describe the individuals inside them.[^robinson50] Whether drought drives hotspots at the level of individual peat domes is not something these tests can say.

On the events themselves, the 136 depend on three parameters (1.5 km, 30 days, at least 3 points) for which this post reports no sensitivity analysis. Density-based clustering grows a cluster outward from any point with enough neighbours,[^ester96] so a chain of nearby groups can merge into a single long event; the 55-day event 9 may be one smouldering fire, or several linked ones. A small grid over distance and time would show how stable the event count and the 8-of-10 early-season result are.

### Lead times: what the ENSO literature suggests

The lag search stopped at 6 months, with two optima on the boundary. Wooster and colleagues found, for Borneo before the MODIS era, that the strongest association across five El Niño events was between fire count and the 16-month *cumulative* Niño 3 anomaly (r = 0.98), and suggested that ENSO forecasts could give months of lead.[^wooster12] That is a different quantity from ONI at a single lag, and it argues for extending the search beyond six months and for trying accumulated anomalies. Their data are Borneo, 1980–2000 and five events, so the number is a pointer and not a benchmark.

Aldrian and Susanto found that Indonesia's rainfall regions differ in how strongly ENSO shapes them, and that March–May is the hardest season to predict.[^aldrian03] Events that begin in February and March sit at the edge of that season, which is consistent with the post's advice to lean on local drought rather than ONI for early-season alerts. Differences in lead *between* the eleven kecamatan are, I think, more likely to be statistical noise from two El Niño episodes than distinct regimes, because the units are small and adjacent; that is my reading, not a result.

### Beyond ENSO: the Indian Ocean Dipole and non-drought fires

Saji and colleagues identified the Indian Ocean dipole mode,[^saji99] and Hendon showed that Indonesian rainfall variability reflects local air-sea interaction as well as ENSO.[^hendon03] For fire, the consequences are documented. A strong positive dipole from mid-July to mid-December 2019 reduced rainfall over Indonesia and favoured peat fires in southern Sumatra,[^iskandar22] and a 1995–2016 analysis for South Sumatra found the largest hotspot counts in 2015 and in the 2006 positive-dipole year.[^putra19] That bears directly on 2019, which holds 24% of the decade's hotspots. A dipole index added to the lag analysis is the obvious next step.

Fire can also arrive without a drought at all. The June 2013 Sumatran fires burned 163,336 ha, 84% of it peat, in a year wetter than average.[^gaveau14] The three large Bengkalis events in 2018, 2020 and 2021 are not 2013, but they are a reminder that the "ENSO year or not" frame is incomplete.

## What to take from this

<div class="pp-callout"><div>
<p><strong>Time, not space, is where the structure is.</strong> The data support a declining trend, strong seasonality, event-like bursts and place-specific lead times. They do not support claims about spatial hotspots that persist, intensify or spread.</p>
<p><strong>Plan for the early dry spell.</strong> The biggest events in the record started in February–March, before the main dry season.</p>
<p><strong>Use two signals.</strong> ONI gives months of notice in some kecamatan; SPEI-3 gives the sharper, near-term signal in several. They are complements, not alternatives.</p>
</div></div>

For how much of this predictive skill is climate, see [the feature ablation](/blog/climate-vs-everything-else-ablation-bengkalis). For whether hotspots measure fire at all, see [the burned-area validation](/blog/hotspot-vs-burned-area-validation-bengkalis).

<div class="pp-callout note"><div>
<p><strong>Limits.</strong> Eleven kecamatan make eleven spatial units, which limits any spatial statistic: the notebook itself notes that the island kecamatan need special handling in the neighbour weights. ST-DBSCAN uses only the high-confidence hotspots (2,292 of 3,402), so smouldering fires that the main analysis rescues are not in these events. The ten-year record contains only two El Niño episodes (2015–16 and 2023–24), so lead-time estimates may not be stable.</p>
</div></div>
