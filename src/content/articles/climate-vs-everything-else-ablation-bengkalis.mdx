A fire model with 14 predictors can tell you how well it does. It cannot, on its own, tell you where its skill comes from. The revised thesis notebook asks it directly: how much of the prediction is climate, and how much is everything else (peat, land use, people)? It answers by removing features in groups and re-scoring the model each time, with a validation scheme designed so that the answer cannot be flattered by spatial leakage.

## The short version

- **Climate carries most of the skill.** Five climate variables alone reach a pooled AUC of 0.727. Removing them from the full model drops it to 0.703, the largest loss of any group, and the model gets worse in all 11 kecamatan.
- **Static features cannot say *when*.** Peat area, depth, oil-palm fraction and the like are constant in a kecamatan, so they cannot rank months within it. Their per-fold AUC is exactly 0.500 by construction.
- **Adding all non-climate features to climate and season buys little.** The pooled gain is 0.054, but within kecamatan it is 0.004 and not significant (6 of 11 kecamatan improve, p = 0.47).
- **Mandau is not climate-independent.** An earlier reading of this study called it so. The held-out model predicts it better than any other kecamatan (AUC 0.89), and climate carries 61% of the SHAP weight there.

## How the test is built

The target is binary: does a kecamatan-month contain at least one filtered hotspot? That happens in 319 of 1,320 months, a base rate of 24.2%. The model is a random forest with 200 trees.[^breiman01] Validation is **leave-one-kecamatan-out**: train on ten kecamatan, test on the eleventh, repeat eleven times. Randomly splitting months would leak, because neighbouring kecamatan share weather and fire history; holding out a whole kecamatan does not. This follows the standard advice for dependent data: cross-validation that ignores dependence can badly underestimate prediction error, and blocked designs are recommended whenever dependence exists.[^roberts17]

The 14 features come in four groups, listed explicitly in the notebook. Rainfall comes from CHIRPS[^funk15] and air temperature from ERA5-Land.[^munoz21]

Two numbers are reported for every configuration. **Pooled AUC** puts all held-out predictions in one pot and also rewards getting the *ranking of kecamatan* right (Rupat burns more than Mandau). **Per-fold AUC** is the average of eleven separate scores, each measuring only whether the model can tell fire months from quiet months *inside* the held-out kecamatan. The two answer different questions, and the interesting results are where they disagree. The distinction matters because AUC is known to depend strongly on the extent of the area being modelled, and says nothing about the spatial pattern of errors.[^lobo08]

## The ablation

Click any row. The dot is the estimate; the line is a 95% bootstrap interval over the eleven kecamatan, which is wide because eleven is a small number of units. Switch to the per-fold view to see the within-kecamatan skill.

<AblationChart client:visible />

Reading it from the top:

**Seasonality alone is a weak baseline** (0.586), a little better than a coin and below the static features' pooled score. **Climate alone is far better** (0.727), and **climate plus seasonality** gets to 0.741, a gain of 0.155 over seasonality alone (11 of 11 kecamatan improve).

**The static features show the pooled/per-fold split most clearly.** Pooled, they reach 0.624, but the interval (0.496 to 0.725) includes chance. Per-fold, they score exactly 0.500, as they must: a number that never changes within a kecamatan cannot tell you which of its months will burn. Whatever they contribute, it is to the *level* of fire between places, not its timing.

**Dropping a group shows how much the others can cover.** Removing the biophysical-static group changes nothing (0.799, a slight gain). Removing the anthropogenic group costs 0.045 pooled but only 0.002 per-fold. Removing climate costs 0.092 pooled and 0.135 per-fold. No other group comes close.

**ONI adds little on top of the local drought variables.** The full model without ONI scores 0.786, against 0.795 with it. The difference is small (+0.009), and within kecamatan only eight of eleven do better with it (p = 0.054).

**The simple model is close to the forest.** Logistic regression on all features reaches a pooled 0.806, nominally above the forest (−0.012 for the forest, interval spanning zero), although within kecamatan the forest is ahead in 10 of 11 (+0.023 on average). With 11 kecamatan, the case for a more flexible model is weak.

The fourth row is the honest summary of what the non-climate variables add. Across kecamatan the pooled difference is just significant, because the static features help with the between-place ranking. Within a kecamatan, where the early-warning question lives, the gain is 0.004 and indistinguishable from noise. The notebook flags the caveat itself: with n = 11 the intervals are wide and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test[^wilcoxon45] has little power.

## Where the model's attention goes

SHAP values split each prediction into contributions from the features. Summed by group and averaged over held-out predictions, they give a second view of the same question. The SHAP values here are the tree-model version described by Lundberg and colleagues.[^lundberg20] It is a second view of the same fitted model rather than an independent test, so it can reinforce the ablation but cannot replace it.

<ShapShare client:visible />

Climate takes 59% of the weight overall, anthropogenic features 22%, biophysical-static 10% and seasonal 9%. The split is much the same in Mandau (61% climate) and Pinggir (62%).

One correction is built into these numbers. An earlier version of the notebook assigned features to groups by matching keywords, which put air temperature, month and the dry-season flag into "non-climate". With the explicit whitelist the climate share for Mandau is 61.3%, where the keyword version had shown 52.2%.

## Revisiting Mandau

Earlier posts on this blog, [including the vulnerability map](/blog/vulnerability-fire-risk-bengkalis), described Mandau's fire risk as structural, driven by the Duri oil-field operations, deforestation and population density on an agricultural rather than climatic calendar. The revised notebook walks part of that back for Mandau, and its limitations section says so in as many words: the old reading of "r(ONI, hotspots) ≈ 0, climate-independent" is replaced by "dampened coupling, not independence".

Three results drive that change:

- **A model that never saw Mandau predicts it well.** Held out, Mandau scores AUC 0.894, the highest of the 11 (below). A fire regime unrelated to climate would be unlikely to be forecast by a model trained on the other ten.
- **SHAP weight is the same as elsewhere.** 61% climate in Mandau, against 59% overall.
- **The weak ONI correlation has a mundane cause.** Mandau has only 22 filtered hotspots in ten years. With so few events, a bivariate correlation with ONI (ρ = +0.16, p = 0.08) has little power. Its correlation with the SPEI-3 drought index is −0.25 (p = 0.006), weaker than most but inside the range of the other kecamatan (−0.17 to −0.43).

<LokoMap client:visible />

This does not make Mandau an ordinary kecamatan. Its F1 is 0.42, mid-pack among the eleven, and Pinggir's is the lowest (0.17, AUC 0.75; Bukit Batu has the lowest AUC, 0.72). The model ranks Mandau's months well, but at a fixed threshold it still misses many of its fire months. What the evidence supports is narrower than the earlier claim: Mandau's *response to climate is smaller in absolute terms* because it burns so rarely, not that climate is irrelevant. Whether industrial and agricultural activity around Duri adds ignitions is a reasonable hypothesis, and this analysis does not test it.

## What the ablation can and cannot show

Leave-one-kecamatan-out is a good defence against one kind of leakage. It is worth being exact about which kind, and about what the other limits of the design are. This section sets the result against the methods and ecology literature.

### Space is blocked; time is not

Holding out a kecamatan removes spatial dependence between train and test. Ploton and colleagues found that large-scale ecological mapping models performed poorly once validated spatially,[^ploton20] which is why the spatial block is the right default. But it leaves temporal dependence untouched. The ten training kecamatan lived through the same months as the held-out one. ONI is a single series shared by every kecamatan, and gridded rainfall and temperature are likely to be very similar across neighbouring units. A forest can therefore learn that the climate values of September 2015 mean fire, and apply that to the eleventh kecamatan in the same September.

That is a legitimate test of whether the model transfers *across space*. It is a weaker test of whether the model would warn of an episode it has not seen, so the v3 notebook adds the missing run: **leave-one-year-out**, with every prediction made for a year the model never saw. Judged against a climatology baseline (the typical fire rate of that kecamatan and calendar month), the result is sobering. Climate plus season without lags reaches a pooled AUC of 0.724 against 0.679 for climatology, a Brier skill score of only 0.08. Adding lagged climate does not help (0.715). The best variant, which also feeds in the previous month's fire state and the static kecamatan features, reaches 0.814 (Brier skill 0.21), but its static features act as a kecamatan label and two of them were computed on the whole decade, so the notebook flags a mild temporal leak. Year by year the best model beats climatology in seven of ten years. Holding out year and kecamatan together gives AUC 0.793 against 0.524 for climatology, which is more encouraging. The honest reading: the model transfers well across space, and it clearly beats the calendar, but how much of that is climate that can be forecast, rather than persistence of fire from the month before, is not established.

### AUC and the base rate

Lobo and colleagues list the limits of AUC: it ignores the predicted probability values and the model's fit, shows nothing about where errors fall, and responds strongly to the extent of the modelled area.[^lobo08] The pooled figure is exactly the case where extent matters, since it also rewards ranking kecamatan correctly. With 24.2% of months positive, the data are imbalanced enough that Saito and Rehmsmeier's argument for precision-recall plots over ROC applies.[^saito15] The only precision-type numbers reported are the F1 scores (0.42 for Mandau, 0.17 for Pinggir). A per-kecamatan precision-recall curve, drawn against each kecamatan's own base rate, would show how far above climatology the model really is. An early-warning system also needs calibrated probabilities, and AUC says nothing about those.

### Correlated predictors

The five climate variables are not independent. SPI-3 and SPEI-3 are both built around accumulated rainfall, and SPEI differs by adding temperature-driven evaporative demand.[^vicente10] Strobl and colleagues proposed a conditional scheme for random-forest variable importance precisely because standard importance is distorted by correlated predictors.[^strobl08] Group ablation sidesteps that problem: removing the whole group asks whether any climate information is needed, instead of dividing credit among near-duplicates. By the same logic, "ONI adds little" is a statement about its contribution *given* local drought variables. That is what one expects if ENSO acts through local rainfall, as in the El Niño-induced drought that drove 2015,[^field16] and it leaves ONI's value as an upstream indicator untouched.

### Windows and nonlinearity

Fanin and van der Werf found that the rainfall accumulation window that best tracked annual fire differed by region: 120 days in southern Sumatra and Kalimantan, but 30 days in northern Sumatra, with high-fire years separated by antecedent-rainfall thresholds and a nonlinear relationship.[^fanin17] Monthly rainfall (30 days) and the three-month indices (about 90 days) sit at the shorter end of that range, and no 120-day accumulation is among the features, so window choice is a testable gap. On nonlinearity, the near-tie between logistic regression and the forest is notable given how often a threshold response is reported. One possible reason, which this analysis does not test, is that ten years contain too few extreme-drought months for the forest to identify the threshold.

### "Static" is not "non-climate" or "human"

The group labels deserve care in both directions. The climate group is purely meteorological. Taufik and colleagues found, for Borneo, that models including hydrology predicted burnt area better than climate-only models,[^taufik17] and the present model has no water-table or soil-moisture series; its surface-water layer is a long-term occurrence map. The ablation therefore cannot say how the climate share would change if peat hydrology were added, though a dynamic hydrological variable would likely be partly redundant with the climate group and share its credit.

The anthropogenic group is the opposite problem: it is measured once and averaged over a kecamatan. In a random-forest analysis of Borneo fire frequency, Sloan and colleagues found fire rising with oil-palm cover up to about 20% of a grid cell, most strongly within 10 km of plantations.[^sloan17] A once-measured, kecamatan-wide oil-palm fraction cannot encode proximity within 10 km, nor its change over time. Land use does change: across the peatlands of Peninsular Malaysia, Sumatra and Borneo combined, managed land rose from 11% to 33% to 50% of the peatland area between 1990, 2007 and 2015.[^miettinen16] Concessions were also responsible for about 41% of Sumatra's fire emissions in the 2006 burning season.[^marlier15] The finding that non-climate features add 0.004 per fold is therefore a result about *these layers at this resolution*, not about human drivers in general.

A Sumatra-wide ensemble of machine-learning models on 2012–2019 hotspots found the normalized burn ratio to be the strongest predictor in every ENSO phase, followed by human-related factors, and Riau to be the most fire-prone province.[^prasetyo22] That study worked at a different spatial scale with a vegetation-state predictor that the present model lacks, so the two are not directly comparable. It does point to a feature class missing here: a dynamic measure of fuel or vegetation condition.

### Eleven units and the Wilcoxon floor

With 11 pairs and no ties, the smallest two-sided p an exact Wilcoxon test can return is 2 divided by 2 to the power 11, about 0.001. That is the value in every "11/11" row of the table, so 0.001 there marks the floor of what the test can say, not a measured difference in strength between rows. Treating the kecamatan, not the 120 months within each, as the unit of analysis is also what avoids pseudoreplication, the use of inferential statistics on observations that are not independent.[^hurlbert84] It is the reason the intervals are wide, and the right price to pay.

## What to take from this

<div class="pp-callout"><div>
<p><strong>For early warning:</strong> invest in climate monitoring. SPEI-3, SPI-3 and rainfall, with ONI as a lead indicator, deliver nearly all the month-to-month skill the data contains. A model with only these five variables and the calendar is within 0.004 AUC of the full one per fold (0.054 pooled).</p>
<p><strong>For prioritising places:</strong> static variables describe where fire concentrates but not when it will happen. Use them to rank kecamatan, not to time alerts.</p>
<p><strong>For the thesis:</strong> the ablation turns a vague "climate matters most" into a number with an interval, and replaces the Mandau exception with a more modest and better-supported statement.</p>
</div></div>

<div class="pp-callout note"><div>
<p><strong>Limits.</strong> Eleven kecamatan are eleven units, so intervals are wide. The target is "any hotspot", which the previous post tests against burned area. The static features were measured once and apply to the whole decade, so changes in land use over time cannot show up as skill. And ablation reports what the model uses, not what causes fire: a variable with no apparent effect may simply be redundant with another.</p>
