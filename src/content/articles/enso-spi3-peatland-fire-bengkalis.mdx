Bengkalis Regency sits on one of the largest peat landscapes in Riau, and in the last decade it has burned in pulses: a catastrophic 2015,[^field16][^huijnen16] a heavy 2019,[^iskandar22] and long quiet stretches in between. This post walks through what a ten-year record of satellite hotspots can and cannot say about why. It follows the manuscript, adds interactive versions of its figures, and states plainly where the evidence is strong and where it is thin.

## The short version

- **El Niño months carry most of the fire.** Mean hotspots per kecamatan-month are 0.5 in La Niña, 1.8 in Neutral and 4.8 in El Niño. The median is zero in all three, so the effect lives in the tail: a few extreme months do the damage.
- **The signal is real but modest.** ENSO phase explains about 6% of the variance. Peat condition, drainage and ignition sources matter too, and this study does not measure them.
- **A local drought index tracks fire better than the Pacific index.** SPI-3 (3-month standardized precipitation) correlates more strongly with hotspots than ONI does in nearly every kecamatan.
- **Two kecamatan are only weakly tied to ENSO.** Mandau and Pinggir show no significant ONI link after correction. Pinggir, with 353 hotspots, is the clearest case, and the only one where SPI-3 fails as well.

## Where the data comes from

Hotspots are NASA FIRMS MODIS detections for 2015–2024 inside Bengkalis, 5,386 points in total after dropping detections the product flags as something other than vegetation fire (type ≠ 0). Raw satellite fire data contains false alarms (gas flares, refineries, hot ground), so the manuscript filters it in three tiers before any statistics are run.[^giglio16] The product's own confidence value runs from 0 to 100, with low, nominal and high classes cut at 30% and 80%.[^modisguide]

Anchors are grouped with DBSCAN (1.5 km radius, haversine distance), giving 137 fire clusters.[^ester96] A medium-confidence point is kept only if it lies within 1.5 km of an anchor cluster, on the reasoning that smouldering peat produces weaker, patchier detections right next to confirmed fire. Everything else, 1,984 points, is discarded.

The filter is a judgement call, and the numbers move with it. Keeping everything with confidence of at least 30% gives 4,795 points; at least 50% gives 3,655; at least 80% gives only 1,648. The anchor-plus-rescue rule sits between the permissive and strict extremes. The conclusions below use the 3,402-point set.

### Try the filter yourself

The explorer below runs the same filter on all 5,386 raw detections. Step through the five stages, then move the two sliders (the confidence threshold for anchors and the radius) and watch what is kept. Zoom in to see how a candidate point gets rescued by an anchor next to it.

<DbscanExplorer client:visible />

Two things stand out when you play with it.

**The result is fairly stable.** Across anchor thresholds from 60% to 90% the kept set ranges from 3,006 to 3,602 points, and across radii from 0.5 km to 5 km it ranges from 3,020 to 3,609. The study's 3,402 sits inside that band, so the choice of 70% and 1.5 km moves the total by roughly −12% to +6%, not by orders of magnitude.

**The clustering itself does not decide what is kept.** With a minimum of one point per cluster, DBSCAN reduces to chaining anchors that are within the radius of each other. The cluster count swings widely (915 clusters at 0.5 km, 31 at 5 km), but the kept set depends only on the confidence tiers and on how far each candidate is from its nearest anchor. The clusters are useful for describing fire events, and the rescue rule is what shapes the dataset.

## Where it burns

Explore the map below. Switch between the hotspot count and the two climate correlations, filter by year, and click a kecamatan.

<KecMap client:visible />

Rupat (976) and Bandar Laksamana (641) lead, followed by Bengkalis (356) and Pinggir (353). That ranking tracks peat: Rupat is a low-lying island whose interior is peat dome. In ten of the eleven kecamatan, between 82% and 98% of hotspots fall on mapped peat. The exception is Talang Muandau, where only 100 of 180 (56%) do. Peat concentrates burning in the wider region too: across Sumatra and Kalimantan, peatlands cover about 13% of the land but accounted for roughly five times the annual burned share of non-peat land between 2001 and 2018.[^vetrita19] What makes peat different is the fuel: fire burns into the organic soil itself, so peat fires release large carbon stocks that took millennia to accumulate.[^pagehooijer16]

## When it burns

<PhaseTimeline client:visible />

Fire is dominated by a handful of episodes. 2015 alone produced 1,101 hotspots (32% of the decade) and 2015–2016 together produced 49%. The single worst month was July 2015 with 364 hotspots. 2019 is the second wave, with 249 hotspots in March alone. The quietest year was 2022, a La Niña year, with 23.

The calendar view shows a pattern that surprised me.

<SeasonProfile client:visible />

Hotspots peak twice: February to April, and again in July and August. Roughly 54% of all hotspots fall between February and April, mostly in March. About 61% of the hotspots in February and March come from just two years, 2015 and 2019. This is a regency with a bimodal rainfall regime, and the first dry spell of the year matters as much as the better-known mid-year one.[^aldrian03] A fire-season definition built only on August to October would miss most of what happened here.

## Does ENSO sort the months?

The test is a Kruskal-Wallis comparison of monthly hotspot counts across the three ENSO phases,[^kw52] using the NOAA rule (ONI at or beyond ±0.5 °C for at least five consecutive months). ONI is built from the ERSST.v5 sea-surface-temperature product.[^huang17] Counts are zero-inflated and heavily skewed, which is why a rank-based test is used instead of ANOVA.

<PhaseBars client:visible />

The overall test is decisive (H = 77.90, p ≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁷, n = 1,320 kecamatan-months), and the effect size is small (η² = 0.058).[^tomczak14] The medians do not move. The mean rises roughly ninefold from La Niña to El Niño, and the 95th percentile rises from 2 to 26 hotspots per kecamatan-month.

A technical point worth knowing: 76% of kecamatan-months record zero hotspots, so ranks are heavily tied. Dunn's test has to use the tie-corrected variance, and without it the p-values come out far too large. With the correction, all three phase pairs differ.[^dunn64][^dinno15][^bh95]

Splitting by season changes the story slightly. The ONI-hotspot association holds in both the June–September dry season (ρ = +0.24, η² = 0.034) and the rest of the year (ρ = +0.23, η² = 0.079). ENSO phase explains proportionally more of the variation outside the dry season. A plausible reading is that El Niño works partly by switching off the rainy season's normal suppression of fire, rather than only by deepening the dry season.

## Drought beats the Pacific index

SPI-3 is computed from CHIRPS rainfall for each kecamatan.[^funk15][^svoboda12] Hotspots correlate negatively with it (drier means more fire), and the correlation is stronger than the one with ONI in ten of the eleven kecamatan (Bukit Batu is the exception).

<CorrelationDots client:visible />

Pooled across all kecamatan-months, ONI correlates with hotspots at ρ = +0.231 and SPI-3 at ρ = −0.293. Eight of the eleven kecamatan show significant correlations with both indices after false-discovery-rate correction. The regency's total monthly count correlates more strongly with ONI (ρ = +0.36) than any pooled figure suggests, because summing eleven noisy series removes much of the noise.

SPI-3 being the stronger predictor fits the physical picture. ONI describes a remote ocean state, and rainfall over Bengkalis is only one of the things it influences. SPI-3 measures the local deficit that actually dries the peat. Other work in Indonesia also treats accumulated rainfall as the relevant quantity, and finds that the best accumulation window differs by region: about 120 days in southern Sumatra and Kalimantan, about 30 days in northern Sumatra.[^fanin17]

### Mandau and Pinggir

Mandau has few hotspots (22), so its weak ONI correlation (ρ = +0.16, not significant after correction) is partly a power problem. Its SPI-3 link survives correction (ρ = −0.24, adjusted p ≈ 0.013), so the coupling is dampened rather than absent.

Pinggir is different. With 353 hotspots there is plenty to analyse, yet neither ONI (ρ = +0.15, adjusted p ≈ 0.11) nor SPI-3 (ρ = −0.15, adjusted p ≈ 0.11) is significant. Its fires look driven by something other than the weather. The manuscript points to land-use conversion and drainage from decades of plantation and oil-field development as candidates, but those factors are not measured here, so this stays a hypothesis.

## Reading the result against the wider literature

Four points from published work sharpen how the numbers above should be read. They are context for the findings, not part of the manuscript's own analysis.

**The dryness–fire link is nonlinear, which a rank correlation understates.** A study of Indonesia from 1997 to 2015 attributes the nonlinear rainfall–fire relationship to longer rain-free spells in extremely dry years.[^fanin17] Fire on degraded peat has been shown to stay nonlinearly sensitive to dry conditions, including in 2015.[^field16] A Borneo analysis found fire frequency jumping when monthly rainfall fell below roughly 200 mm.[^sloan17] That fits what this post found: medians of zero in every ENSO phase, and an effect that lives in the tail. A Spearman coefficient and an η² summarise average association, so they will read "modest" for a process that behaves like a threshold. A natural next step is to test exceedance models, for example the probability of a fire month once accumulated rainfall drops below a regionally estimated level, and to compare accumulation windows rather than fixing SPI-3.

**Fire does not need an ENSO year.** Fires in central Sumatra in June 2013 burned an estimated 163,336 ha, 84% of it peatland and about 82% on deforested land, in a year that was wetter than average and after only a two-month dry spell.[^gaveau14] The 2019 season, which this post flags as the second wave despite a weak ENSO signal, coincided with a strong positive Indian Ocean Dipole that cut rainfall across Indonesia and raised hotspot numbers in southern Sumatra.[^iskandar22] An earlier South Sumatra study found the largest hotspot counts in 1995–2016 in the 2006 positive-dipole year and in 2015.[^putra19] The Pacific index cannot see this. The standard Indian Ocean index is the dipole mode index defined in the original dipole-mode paper,[^saji99] and adding it as a second climate covariate is a direct test of whether 2019 is explained.

**The early-year peak sits where ENSO is weakest.** A regionalisation of Indonesian rainfall found a north-western region with little ENSO signal, and found March to May to be the hardest season to predict.[^aldrian03] Indonesian rainfall variability reflects local air–sea interaction as well as ENSO.[^hendon03] The February–April hotspot peak documented above falls in that window, which suggests that ENSO's weaker explanatory power outside the June–September season may reflect the local rainfall regime as much as El Niño "switching off" rainy-season suppression. Whether Bengkalis lies inside the north-western region should be checked against the regionalisation map before leaning on it.

**Pinggir's "something else" has candidate causes that can be tested.** Concessions accounted for about 41% of fire emissions in Sumatra in the 2006 burning season, most of it from peat, and timber concessions held 47% of Sumatra's concession area but 88% of its concession-related fire emissions.[^marlier15] In Borneo, fire frequency rose with oil palm expansion, most strongly within 10 km of plantations.[^sloan17] Hydrology matters too: in Borneo, models that include subsurface hydrology predicted burnt area better than climate-only models.[^taufik17] These studies do not concern Pinggir directly. They give a concrete overlay list for the manuscript's hypothesis: concession boundaries, canal density, and peat-restoration or rewetting records.

## What this means for early warning

An ENSO-based alert is useful for Bengkalis, with caveats. ONI forecasts give months of lead time, and El Niño months concentrate the extreme fire events. Because ENSO phase explains so little on its own, it should raise a risk level rather than trigger one, and be combined with local SPI-3 monitoring. Pinggir calls for a different approach: land-use enforcement, canal and drainage management, and ignition history should weigh more than climate forecasts there.

## Caveats

<div class="pp-callout"><div>
<p><strong>Pooling inflates confidence.</strong> The 1,320 kecamatan-months are not independent. All eleven kecamatan respond to the same ONI value in a given month, so the real number of independent climate samples is closer to the 120 months. Read the p-values as evidence of direction, not as precise error rates.</p>
<p><strong>Hotspots are not burned area.</strong> A hotspot is a satellite detection of heat at roughly 1 km resolution. It is a good indicator of fire activity and a poor measure of fire size.</p>
<p><strong>The filter shapes the counts.</strong> The confidence thresholds and the 1.5 km radius were chosen on judgement and were not tuned against ground-truth fire records.</p>
<p><strong>SPI-3 depends on its baseline.</strong> It is standardized against 1991–2024 (34 years of CHIRPS rainfall), which meets the 30-year guidance. The draft manuscript used 2015–2024 only, so SPI-3 values here differ from the draft and should not be compared with it.</p>
<p><strong>The correlations are bivariate.</strong> No land cover, drainage or population variables are controlled for.</p>
</div></div>

Each caveat has support in the methods literature. Treating non-independent observations as independent replicates is the classic problem of pseudoreplication.[^hurlbert84] A hotspot count is a weak guide to fire size, and the conversion to hectares varies with site and vegetation.[^tansey08] The confidence cut-offs follow the product's own classes only partly, and the algorithm's omission of small fires is large,[^giglio16] so the filter is better read as a sensitivity range than a single truth. For SPI, the WMO user guide asks for a long calibration record. A summary of that guidance from the Integrated Drought Management Programme gives 20 years as the absolute minimum and 30 years as the ideal,[^svoboda12] which the 1991–2024 baseline meets; the earlier 2015–2024 baseline did not.

Every figure above is drawn from the 3,402 filtered points of the thesis pipeline (rerun of 16 October 2026). Hotspot counts, phase statistics, Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn, and the ONI and SPI-3 correlations were recomputed from that run; the SPI-3 values come from CHIRPS with a 1991–2024 baseline, and the rainfall series itself is not part of the public data repository. ONI values are the series downloaded in that run (NOAA PSL); they differ from the draft manuscript's series by up to 0.19 °C, which gives 44 El Niño months instead of 43.
